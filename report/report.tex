\begin{abstract}
We predict whether an AI system answers a benchmark item correctly. The test benchmarks are not in the training
data, and labels come a few at a time: 0, 1, 3, 7, 15 and 31 per system and benchmark. For each test benchmark we fit
a logistic item response model at test time, using the labels of all systems on that benchmark. Item effects have a
Gaussian process prior, so items with close text get close difficulty. We average the model over a grid of prior
scales, with weights from the Laplace marginal likelihood. Two things mattered most. First, with few labels most of
the error comes from not knowing how well a system does on the benchmark overall, and our prior for this level was
centred at the wrong value. Training groups of the size used at test time have a mean accuracy near 0.36, and the
test groups we could read from the formative feedback have 0.35. Moving the centre of the prior to this level lowers
the Brier score at budget 0 from 0.248 to 0.236 and the Brier ALC by 0.002. This is the largest single gain we found.
Second, a linear kernel on item embeddings, centred within each benchmark, is the part of the item kernel that helps
most once a benchmark has a few dozen labels. In a simulation of the protocol on held-out benchmarks, the final model
has a Brier ALC of 0.1797. A group mean gets 0.1869 and a constant 0.5 gets 0.25.
\end{abstract}

\section{Task and data}
A subject is an AI system with fixed settings. An item is one task, with its text, some metadata and an anonymous
benchmark ID. A group is one subject on one benchmark. In each scored group with at least 80 items, the items are
split in two halves. Labels are shown from one half and the other half is scored. Labels come in nested steps of 0,
1, 3, 7, 15 and 31 per group \citep{paiec2026}. The predictor sees the labels of every group, also of groups it does not predict. The
score is the Brier ALC \citep{brier1950}, $0.1B_0+0.2B_1+0.2B_3+0.2B_7+0.2B_{15}+0.1B_{31}$, averaged over groups.

We use only the public training pool \texttt{aims-foundations/measurement-db} \citep{measurementdb2026} (revision \texttt{54074453}). We keep
benchmarks with binary answers per item and \texttt{release = public}, which gives 160 benchmarks. Of these, 92 have
at least one group with 80 to 1000 items. This gives 10{,}208 groups, 154{,}628 items and 4.3M subject and item
cells. Each cell has $n$ trials and $k$ successes. We use no other evaluation data.

\section{Simulating the protocol offline}\label{sec:sim}
The test benchmarks never appear in training. So we made every choice with a simulator of the official protocol and
5-fold cross-validation over benchmarks.

\paragraph{Draws.} We add groups one at a time. Groups whose benchmark or model name is already in the draw go last,
and ties are broken at random. We stop when the draw would go over 1000 subject and item pairs. We use three
settings. The realistic setting is our main one. It only allows groups with at most 200 items. A draw then has on
average 6.9 groups, 5.9 benchmarks and 142 items per group. The large group setting allows all groups with 80 to 1000
items and gives about 3 groups per draw. In the many subjects setting the limit counts distinct items, so one
benchmark can have hundreds of subjects.

\paragraph{Split, labels and scoring.} In each group we split the distinct items at random, and the odd item goes to
the label pool. Labels come in random order, with one sampled trial per item, and every group sees the labels of all
other groups. We compute the exact Brier score of each group over all response rows, apply the ALC weights, and
average over groups and then over episodes. We always compare two models on the same episodes, with the same splits
and the same labels. We report the mean of the paired differences and its standard error. We chose settings on one
seed and checked them again on episodes drawn with another seed.

\paragraph{What the formative feedback tells us.} Codabench \citep{xu2022codabench} scores each submission on a new draw of about nine
groups. The scoring log gives the Brier score and the calibration error (ECE) of every group at every budget. Since
the draw changes each time, these logs say little about which model is better. But they do tell us what the test
groups look like. A draw has about nine groups from seven benchmarks, with 88 to 152 items per group. This is what
our realistic setting gives. The logs also give the accuracy of each group. Our first three submissions predicted
exactly 0.5 at budget 0. In that case the ECE of a group is $|q-0.5|$, where $q$ is its accuracy. Later submissions
predicted a little below 0.5 at budget 0, so their Brier score is above 0.25 only when $q>0.5$, and this tells us the
sign. On the 27 groups we recovered this way, the mean accuracy is 0.35. Figure~\ref{fig:acc} compares them with the
training groups. The test groups look like the training groups of realistic size (mean 0.36 per group, 0.39 per
benchmark). They do not look like the training pool as a whole (0.49 per benchmark), where large benchmarks with
many subjects weigh the most. So the logs told us which setting to use, and that the level of the shift prior was
worth testing. We then chose the value of every parameter, including the centre of that prior, by cross-validation
on the training pool.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Model}
Fix a benchmark $b$ and let $\mathcal{L}$ be the labels shown on $b$, from any subject. For subject $s$ and item $t$,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{logit} P(y_{st}=1) = c_t + m + a_s + d_t,\quad
a_s\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_a^2),\; d\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,\sigma_d^2 K).
\end{equation}
Here $c_t$ is a fixed offset from a prior model (Section~\ref{sec:prior}), $m$ is a shift for the benchmark, $a_s$ is
a shift for the subject, and $d_t$ is the item effect, which is the same for all subjects of the benchmark.
This is a logistic item response model \citep{lord1980} with a Gaussian process prior on the item effects
\citep{rasmussen2006}. Models of this family are also used to evaluate language models on few items
\citep{maiapolo2024}. Section~\ref{sec:shift} describes the prior on $m$. The item kernel is
\begin{equation}
K = (1-\rho_\ell)\big[\rho\,e^{(\cos(e_i,e_j)-1)/\ell} + \rho_f\,\kappa_{\text{tok}}(i,j) + (1-\rho-\rho_f)\,\mathbb{1}[i=j]\big]
  + \rho_\ell\,\frac{\tilde e_i^\top \tilde e_j}{\overline{\|\tilde e\|^2}}.
\end{equation}
$e$ is the normalised \texttt{multilingual-e5-small} \citep{wang2024e5} embedding of the item text and metadata. $\kappa_{\text{tok}}$ is
the cosine similarity of \texttt{key=value} metadata tokens, where we only count tokens found on at least two labeled
items. $\tilde e = e-\bar e_{\mathcal{L}}$ is the embedding minus its mean over the labeled items of the benchmark.
The last term is a Bayesian linear regression of item difficulty on the embedding. It is learned for each benchmark
from its own labels.

\subsection{Inference}
We find the posterior mode with Newton's method. The Hessian block of the subject effects is diagonal, so we remove
it with a Schur complement. What is left is a block for $m$ and the labeled item effects. Its size is at most the
number of distinct labeled items. One Newton step costs $O(|\mathcal{L}| + n_s n_{\mathcal{L}} + n_{\mathcal{L}}^3)$.
The Laplace posterior \citep[Chapter~3]{rasmussen2006} gives a mean $\mu$ and a variance $v$ of the logit for any target item, labeled or not, through
the Gaussian process conditional of $d_t$ given $d_{\mathcal{L}}$. We predict
$\sigma\big(\mu/\sqrt{1+\pi\gamma v/8}\big)$. With $\gamma=1$ this is the usual probit approximation of the
predictive integral \citep{mackay1992}. We use $\gamma=0.35$ while the target subject has at most three labels on the benchmark, and
$\gamma=0.2$ after that. We chose these values in simulation (Section~\ref{sec:risk}). We checked our code against a
dense Laplace solver, and the two agree to machine precision.

\subsection{Averaging over prior scales}
Good prior scales are very different from one benchmark to the next. On some benchmarks items are almost always right
or always wrong. On others the item effects are small. So we fit the model for every combination of
$\sigma_a\in\{0.4,0.8,1.6\}$, $\sigma_d\in\{0.8,1.6,3.2\}$ and the three parts of the shift prior below, with
$\rho=0.73$, $\rho_f=0.17$, $\ell=0.12$ and $\rho_\ell=0.4$. We average the predicted probabilities with weights
proportional to the Laplace marginal likelihood, as in Bayesian model averaging \citep{hoeting1999}. We do this for each benchmark and each set of shown labels on its
own.

\subsection{Prior on the benchmark shift}\label{sec:shift}
The shift $m$ sets how well a subject does overall on a new benchmark. Its prior is a mixture with equal weights,
\begin{equation}
m \sim \tfrac13\big[\mathcal{N}(\mu_0, 0.8^2) + \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, 1.6^2) + \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, 3.2^2)\big],
\qquad \mu_0=-0.6,
\end{equation}
and each part is one more axis of the averaging grid. The wide part lets the model follow benchmarks where a subject
gets almost everything right or almost everything wrong. The narrow part keeps predictions stable with one or two
labels. The centre $\mu_0$ matters more than the scales. In training groups of realistic size the mean logit
accuracy is $-0.87$, with a standard deviation of 1.8. So a prior centred at zero starts every new benchmark from an
accuracy that is too high. We set $\mu_0$ by cross-validation (Section~\ref{sec:results}). The value we chose is
between zero and the training mean. Two things explain why it does not need to reach $-0.87$. The offset $c_t$ has a
negative mean and already carries part of the low level. And the probit correction pulls predictions toward 0.5.

\subsection{Prior across benchmarks and second model}\label{sec:prior}
We take hidden states of Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct \citep{qwen2024} (layer 21 of 28, mean over tokens, standardised) and fit a ridge
regression to the mean logit accuracy of each item, with equal weight for all training benchmarks. The output is
$z_t$, and we set $c_t = 0.3\,z_t$. With no labels the prediction is the prior predictive, averaged over the parts of
the shift prior. The final predictor is the average of two copies of the model. They differ only in the item vectors
used by the kernel: e5-small embeddings in one, Qwen hidden states in the other.

\subsection{Label choice and run time}
We keep the platform's default random choice of labels (Section~\ref{sec:negative}). Each prediction worker keeps one
fit per benchmark and set of labels in a cache. The label list only grows, so we can tell that a set has not changed
from its length and its last entry. Item vectors are cached too. Without a GPU, or if Qwen cannot be loaded, the
predictor uses the e5 model alone with no offset $c_t$. If Qwen fails during the run, we turn it off and go on with
the e5 model. In simulation the e5 model alone is worse by only 0.0003 ALC in the realistic setting. A prediction
takes 15 to 110 ms on the A100 we used for testing.

Table~\ref{tab:main} adds the parts of the model one at a time, on the same 1000 episodes in every row. In the
realistic setting, the paired standard error of the difference between two rows is about $10^{-4}$. The centred
shift prior gives the largest step in the realistic setting ($-0.0022$, from 0.1819 to 0.1797), and almost all of it
comes at budgets 0 and 1. The centred linear kernel gives the largest step with large groups ($-0.0025$) and helps
from budget 7 on. The final model is the best row in the realistic and many subjects settings. With large groups it
is within 0.0003 of the best row, which is inside the noise of that setting. Most of the other rows change the ALC by
less than 0.001.

\paragraph{Where the error comes from.} Two oracles show how far a predictor can go in the realistic setting. If we
knew the accuracy of each group, a constant prediction per group would score 0.1503. If we knew the accuracy of each
item for each subject, the Brier score would be 0.011. Our $B_{31}$ of 0.1494 is just below the first oracle. With 31
labels the model knows the group level well and gets a bit more from the items. But the gap to the second oracle
shows that most of the item signal is still out of reach. The row \emph{Bayes, own labels} in Table~\ref{tab:main} is
a more useful point of comparison. It is the Bayes predictor of the group accuracy from the group's own labels. Its
prior is the accuracy of training groups from other folds (groups of 80 to 200 items, in all three columns). At
budget 0 it predicts the mean training accuracy, which is the best constant under the training distribution. Its
$B_0$ is 0.2356, and the final model gets the same value. So without information on single items, there is little
left to gain at budget 0. From budget 1 on, the final model beats this reference by 0.004 to 0.008 at every budget.
This part of the gain comes from the labels of other subjects on the same benchmark and from the item kernel.

\paragraph{Prior on the benchmark shift.} We compared centres and scales on 300 paired episodes of the realistic
setting, against the model of the row before it in Table~\ref{tab:main}. Centres of $-0.4$, $-0.6$ and $-0.8$ lower
the ALC by 0.0018 to 0.0022, all with a standard error near 0.0002, while $-1.2$ gives only 0.0010. The scales matter
less: $(1, 2, 4)$, $(0.8, 1.6, 3.2)$ and $(1.5, 3)$ are within 0.0001 of each other. Gaussian mixtures with two or
three parts, fit directly to the accuracies of training groups, did about as well ($-0.0023$), so we kept the simpler
prior. A larger weight of 0.5 for the Qwen offset did not add to the gain, and neither did more confident predictions
with few labels. Once the prior is centred, the Qwen offset no longer matters: setting $c_t=0$ changes the ALC by
$+0.00001\pm0.00007$. We kept it anyway, since we had tested the full runtime with it. On 1000 new episodes drawn with
another seed, the final model beats the row before it by $0.0022\pm0.0001$ ALC. The Brier score drops by 0.012 at
budget 0 and by 0.004 at budget 1. In the large group setting, where training accuracy is higher, the centred prior
neither helps nor hurts. The change is $-0.0008\pm0.0005$ on the episodes used for the choice and
$+0.0002\pm0.0003$ on new episodes.

\paragraph{Risk with very few labels.}\label{sec:risk} With one to three labels, a predictor can move away from the
truth. In simulation, $B_3>B_0$ happens for about a quarter of the groups. In the 53 groups of our first formative
logs it happened for 24.5\%, so the simulator matches what we saw on the platform. A smaller variance scale $\gamma$
makes predictions more confident. It lowers the mean ALC down to about $\gamma=0.1$, but it also makes bad groups
more common: the share of groups with $B_3>0.3$ goes from 5.2\% at $\gamma=0.5$ to 7.4\% at $\gamma=0.2$. We kept
$\gamma=0.35$ while the subject has at most three labels, and $\gamma=0.2$ after that, when the posterior knows more.

\paragraph{Formative feedback.} The final model (submission 1018959) scored 0.182 Brier ALC on Codabench, with an
ECE of 0.139. Our seven earlier submissions scored between 0.164 and 0.228. Each submission is scored on a new draw
of eight or nine groups, so these numbers move by several hundredths from one draw to the next, and we did not use
them to rank models.

\section{What did not help}\label{sec:negative}
We tested each of these ideas with the same paired simulation and dropped them.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
\item Predicting budget 0 from item text and subject attributes with LightGBM \citep{ke2017lightgbm} (e5 embeddings, text statistics,
provider, release date, size, model name). The best $B_0$ was 0.248, against 0.250. Once the shift prior is centred,
the model already reaches the $B_0$ of the Bayes reference (0.2356).
\item Asking Qwen2.5-7B to rate item difficulty from 1 to 9. Inside a benchmark, the rank correlation with measured
difficulty was $-0.03$. Items rarely come with a reference answer (3 benchmarks out of 92), so we could not score the
likelihood of the correct answer either.
\item Looking for the same item in other benchmarks. Fewer than 0.1\% of items have a match with cosine above 0.95.
\item Learning a projection of the embeddings for the kernel by maximising the marginal likelihood on training
groups. It was worse on held-out benchmarks. A learned low-rank metric for the linear kernel did not help either.
\item Bigger item vectors (e5-base, e5-large, Qwen2.5-7B layers 14, 21 or 28) as the only kernel input. None beat
e5-small. With so few labels, the model is limited by the number of labels more than by the quality of the vectors.
Using Qwen2.5-14B instead of the 7B model gained 0.0002 ALC and needed twice the memory.
\item Stacking with LightGBM on model outputs and label counts, and Platt scaling \citep{platt2000} per budget. Both were worse on
held-out benchmarks, because calibration learned on some benchmarks does not carry over to others.
\item Other ways to choose labels: uncertainty, overlap with other subjects, embedding diversity, D-optimal design.
None was clearly different from random order.
\item Learning the centre of the shift prior during the run, from the label rates of the other benchmarks in the same
draw (a hierarchical prior with a shared centre). It was worse in every fold, by 0.0001 to 0.0007 ALC depending on
the prior width of the centre, even in the fold with the hardest benchmarks. How well one subject does on one
benchmark says little about how other subjects do on other benchmarks.
\item Weights for the averaging grid learned on training benchmarks. The posterior over the grid stays almost flat
even with 31 labels (weights from 0.026 to 0.048 over 27 points), so a learned prior is close to the uniform one.
\item A prior on subject ability from the same model name on other benchmarks. Its out-of-fold correlation with
ability on held-out benchmarks was $-0.16$, and it gave no gain. A ridge regression of the group level on all
subject metadata tokens did no better. Its out-of-fold correlation with the group logit was between $-0.19$ and
$+0.24$ depending on the subset, and on all groups it was worse than a constant. A prior on item difficulty inside a
benchmark, from a ridge on centred Qwen states (Spearman 0.07), hurt $B_0$ and $B_1$ more than it helped later.
\item Numeric metadata (lengths, counts and similar fields) or text statistics as an extra kernel term. All versions
were 0.0001 to 0.0005 worse.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}
The simulator depends on how we read the draw rule. We matched it to the formative logs, and for this reason we
checked every choice in three settings. The centred shift prior helps because test groups have a low accuracy on
average, like the training groups of the same size. On a test set made mostly of easy benchmarks, it would lose part
of its gain at budget 0, although it did not hurt in our large group setting. The Qwen parts need a GPU at test time.
Without one, the predictor still runs with the e5 model alone. The model assumes that item effects are the same for
all subjects of a benchmark, apart from the logistic link. Last, with no labels the model predicts close to the
average level of a group, because the data we have says little about how well a subject will do on a new benchmark.

Code: \url{https://github.com/nguefackuriel/paiec-gpirt}. The README goes through the full pipeline: download of the
