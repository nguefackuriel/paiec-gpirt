\begin{abstract}
The task is to predict whether an AI system answers a benchmark item correctly. Test benchmarks are not in the
training data, and labels are revealed a few at a time (0, 1, 3, 7, 15 and 31 per subject and benchmark). For each
test benchmark we fit a logistic item response model at test time, pooling the labels of every subject. Item effects
get a Gaussian process prior, so items with similar text get similar difficulty, and we average over a grid of prior
scales with weights from the Laplace marginal likelihood. Two findings shaped the final model. First, most of the
error with few labels comes from not knowing how well the subject does on the benchmark as a whole, and the shift
prior that sets this level was centred at the wrong place. Training groups of the size seen at test time have a mean
accuracy near 0.36, and the test groups we could recover from formative feedback have 0.35. Centring the prior on this level lowers
the Brier score at budget 0 from 0.248 to 0.236 and the Brier ALC by 0.002, the largest single step in our realistic setting. Second, a linear kernel on item
embeddings centred within the benchmark is the part of the item kernel that helps most once a benchmark has a few
dozen labels. In a simulation of the protocol with held-out benchmarks, the final model reaches a Brier ALC of 0.1797,
against 0.1869 for a group mean and 0.25 for a constant 0.5.
\end{abstract}

\section{Task and data}
A subject is an AI system with fixed settings. An item is a task with its text, some metadata and an anonymous
benchmark ID. A group is one subject on one benchmark. For each scored group with at least 80 items, the items are
split in two halves: labels are revealed from one half and the other half is scored. Labels come in nested steps of
0, 1, 3, 7, 15 and 31 per group. The predictor receives the revealed labels of every group, including groups it is
not predicting. The score is the Brier ALC, $0.1B_0+0.2B_1+0.2B_3+0.2B_7+0.2B_{15}+0.1B_{31}$, averaged over
groups.

We use only the public training pool \texttt{aims-foundations/measurement-db} (revision \texttt{54074453}). We keep
benchmarks with binary item-level responses and \texttt{release = public}, which gives 160 benchmarks. Of these, 92
have at least one group with 80 to 1000 items, for 10{,}208 groups, 154{,}628 items and 4.3M subject and item
cells. Each cell holds $n$ trials and $k$ successes. No other evaluation data is used.

\section{Simulating the protocol offline}\label{sec:sim}
Test benchmarks never appear in training, so every choice was made with a simulator of the official protocol and
5-fold cross-validation over benchmarks.

\paragraph{Draws.} Groups are added one at a time. Groups whose benchmark or model name is already in the draw come
last, and ties are broken at random. We stop when the draw would pass 1000 subject and item pairs. We use three
settings. In the realistic setting, our main one, only groups with at most 200 items are allowed. A draw then has on
average 6.9 groups, 5.9 benchmarks and 142 items per group. The large group setting allows all groups with 80 to
1000 items and gives about 3 groups per draw. In the many subjects setting the limit counts distinct items, so a
benchmark can have hundreds of subjects.

\paragraph{Split, labels and scoring.} In each group the distinct items are split at random, with the odd item going
to the label pool. Labels come in random order, one sampled trial per item, and every group sees the labels of all
other groups. We compute the exact Brier score of each group over all response rows, apply the ALC weights, and
average over groups and then over episodes. Two models are always compared on the same episodes, with the same
splits and the same labels, and we report paired mean differences with their standard errors. Settings were chosen
on one seed and checked again on episodes drawn with another.

\paragraph{What the formative feedback tells us.} Each Codabench submission is scored on a new draw of about nine
groups, and the scoring log gives the Brier score and the calibration error (ECE) of every group at every budget.
These logs say little about which model is better, since the draws change. They do describe the test groups. A draw
has about nine groups from seven benchmarks, with 88 to 152 items per group, which is what the realistic setting
produces. The logs also give the accuracy of each group. Our first three submissions predicted exactly 0.5 at budget
0, and in that case the ECE of a group is $|q-0.5|$, where $q$ is its accuracy. Later submissions predicted slightly
below 0.5 at budget 0, so their Brier score is above 0.25 only when $q>0.5$, which gives the side. On the 27 groups
recovered in this way the mean accuracy is 0.35. Figure~\ref{fig:acc} compares them with the training groups. The
test groups look like the training groups of realistic size (mean 0.36 per group, 0.39 per benchmark), and not like
the training pool as a whole (0.49 per benchmark), which is dominated by large benchmarks with many subjects.
The logs pointed us to the realistic setting and to the level of the shift prior as a question worth testing. The
value of every parameter, the centre of that prior included, was then chosen by cross-validation on the training
pool.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Model}
Fix a benchmark $b$ and let $\mathcal{L}$ be the labels revealed on $b$, from any subject. For subject $s$ and item
$t$,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{logit} P(y_{st}=1) = c_t + m + a_s + d_t,\quad
a_s\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_a^2),\; d\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,\sigma_d^2 K).
\end{equation}
Here $c_t$ is a fixed offset from a prior model (Section~\ref{sec:prior}), $m$ is a shift for the benchmark, $a_s$ is
a shift for the subject and $d_t$ is the item effect, shared by all subjects of the benchmark. The prior on $m$ is
described in Section~\ref{sec:shift}. The item kernel is
\begin{equation}
K = (1-\rho_\ell)\big[\rho\,e^{(\cos(e_i,e_j)-1)/\ell} + \rho_f\,\kappa_{\text{tok}}(i,j) + (1-\rho-\rho_f)\,\mathbb{1}[i=j]\big]
  + \rho_\ell\,\frac{\tilde e_i^\top \tilde e_j}{\overline{\|\tilde e\|^2}}.
\end{equation}
$e$ is the normalised \texttt{multilingual-e5-small} embedding of the item text and metadata. $\kappa_{\text{tok}}$ is
the cosine similarity of \texttt{key=value} metadata tokens, counting only tokens that appear on at least two labeled
items. $\tilde e = e-\bar e_{\mathcal{L}}$ is the embedding minus its mean over the labeled items of the benchmark.
The last term is a Bayesian linear regression of item difficulty on the embedding, learned for each benchmark from
its revealed labels.

\subsection{Inference}
We find the posterior mode with Newton's method. The Hessian block of the subject effects is diagonal, so we remove
it with a Schur complement. What is left is a block for $m$ and the labeled item effects, whose size is at most the
number of distinct labeled items. One Newton step costs $O(|\mathcal{L}| + n_s n_{\mathcal{L}} + n_{\mathcal{L}}^3)$.
The Laplace posterior gives a mean $\mu$ and a variance $v$ of the logit for any target item, labeled or not, through
the Gaussian process conditional of $d_t$ given $d_{\mathcal{L}}$. We predict
$\sigma\big(\mu/\sqrt{1+\pi\gamma v/8}\big)$. With $\gamma=1$ this is the usual probit approximation of the
predictive integral. We use $\gamma=0.35$ while the target subject has at most three labels on the benchmark and
$\gamma=0.2$ after that, values chosen in simulation (Section~\ref{sec:risk}). We checked the code against a dense
Laplace solver, and both agree to machine precision.

\subsection{Averaging over prior scales}
The right prior scales differ a lot between benchmarks. On some benchmarks items are almost always right or always
wrong, while on others the item effects are small. So we fit the model for every combination of
$\sigma_a\in\{0.4,0.8,1.6\}$, $\sigma_d\in\{0.8,1.6,3.2\}$ and the three components of the shift prior below, with
$\rho=0.73$, $\rho_f=0.17$, $\ell=0.12$ and $\rho_\ell=0.4$. The predicted probabilities are averaged with weights
proportional to the Laplace marginal likelihood. This is done for each benchmark and each set of revealed labels on
its own.

\subsection{Prior on the benchmark shift}\label{sec:shift}
The shift $m$ sets the overall level of a subject on a new benchmark. Its prior is a mixture with equal weights,
\begin{equation}
m \sim \tfrac13\big[\mathcal{N}(\mu_0, 0.8^2) + \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, 1.6^2) + \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, 3.2^2)\big],
\qquad \mu_0=-0.6,
\end{equation}
and each component is one more axis of the averaging grid. The wide component lets the model follow benchmarks where
a subject gets almost everything right or wrong, and the narrow one keeps predictions stable with one or two labels.
The centre $\mu_0$ matters more than the scales. In the training groups of realistic size the mean logit accuracy is
$-0.87$ with a standard deviation of 1.8, so a prior centred at zero starts every new benchmark from an accuracy that
is too high. We set $\mu_0$ by cross-validation (Section~\ref{sec:results}). The selected value lies between zero and
the training mean. Part of the low level is already carried by the offset $c_t$, whose mean is negative, and the
probit correction pulls predictions toward 0.5, so the centre does not need to reach $-0.87$.

\subsection{Prior across benchmarks and second model}\label{sec:prior}
We take hidden states of Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (layer 21 of 28, mean over tokens, standardised) and fit a ridge
regression to the mean logit accuracy of each item. All training benchmarks get equal weight. The output is $z_t$
and we set $c_t = 0.3\,z_t$. With no labels the prediction is the prior predictive, averaged over the components of
the shift prior. The final predictor is the average of two copies of the model that differ only in the item vectors
used by the kernel: e5-small embeddings in one, Qwen hidden states in the other.

\subsection{Label choice and run time}
We keep the platform's default random choice of labels (Section~\ref{sec:negative}). Each prediction worker caches
one fit per benchmark and set of labels. Since the label list only grows, an unchanged set is detected from its
length and last entry. Item vectors are cached too. Without a GPU, or if Qwen cannot be loaded, the predictor uses the
e5 model alone with no offset $c_t$. If Qwen fails during the run, it is switched off and the run goes on with the e5
model. In simulation the e5 model alone is worse by only 0.0003 ALC in the realistic setting. A prediction takes 15 to 110 ms on the A100 we used for testing.

Table~\ref{tab:main} adds the parts of the model one at a time, on the same 1000 episodes in every row. The paired
standard error of the difference between two rows is about $10^{-4}$ in the realistic setting. The centred shift prior gives the largest step in the realistic setting ($-0.0022$, from 0.1819 to 0.1797), almost
all of it at budgets 0 and 1. The centred linear kernel gives the largest step with large groups ($-0.0025$) and
helps from budget 7 on. The final model is the best row in the realistic and many subjects settings. With large groups it is within 0.0003
of the best row, which is inside the noise of that setting. Most of the other rows move the ALC by less than 0.001.

\paragraph{Where the error comes from.} Two oracles bound what a predictor can reach in the realistic setting. If the
accuracy of each group were known, a constant prediction per group would score 0.1503. If the accuracy of each item
for each subject were known, the Brier score would be 0.011. Our $B_{31}$ of 0.1494 is just below the first oracle.
With 31 labels the model knows the group level well and gets a little more from the items, but the gap to the second
oracle shows how much of the item signal stays out of reach. The row \emph{Bayes, own labels} of Table~\ref{tab:main} is a more useful reference. It is the Bayes predictor of
the group accuracy from the group's own labels, with the accuracies of training groups from other folds as its prior (groups of 80 to 200 items, in all three
columns). At budget 0
it predicts the mean training accuracy, which is the best constant under the training distribution. Its $B_0$ is
0.2356 and the final model reaches the same value, so little is left to gain at budget 0 without information on
single items. From budget 1 on the final model is better than this reference
by 0.004 to 0.008 at every budget. This part of the gain comes from the labels of other subjects on the same benchmark
and from the item kernel. Figure~\ref{fig:curves} shows the curves.

\paragraph{Prior on the benchmark shift.} We compared centres and scales on 300 paired episodes of the realistic
setting, against the model of the previous row of Table~\ref{tab:main}. Centres of $-0.4$, $-0.6$ and $-0.8$ lower the
ALC by 0.0018 to 0.0022, all with a standard error near 0.0002, while $-1.2$ gives only 0.0010. The scales matter less:
$(1, 2, 4)$, $(0.8, 1.6, 3.2)$ and $(1.5, 3)$ are within 0.0001 of each other. Gaussian mixtures with two or three
components fit directly to the training group accuracies did about as well ($-0.0023$), so we kept the simpler
prior. Raising the weight of the Qwen offset to 0.5 or making predictions more confident with few labels did not add
to the gain. Once the prior is centred, the Qwen offset itself no longer matters: setting $c_t=0$ changes the ALC by
$+0.00001\pm0.00007$. We kept it, since the runtime was checked end to end with it. On 1000 new episodes drawn with another seed, the final model is better than the previous row by
$0.0022\pm0.0001$ ALC, of which 0.012 at budget 0 and 0.004 at budget 1. In the large group setting, where the training accuracy is higher, the centred prior neither helps nor hurts: the
change is $-0.0008\pm0.0005$ on the episodes used for the choice and $+0.0002\pm0.0003$ on new episodes.

\paragraph{Risk with very few labels.}\label{sec:risk} With one to three labels, a predictor can move away from the
truth. In simulation, $B_3>B_0$ happens for about a quarter of the groups, and in the 53 groups of our first formative
logs it happened for 24.5\%, so the simulator matches the real behaviour on this point. A smaller variance scale
$\gamma$ makes predictions more confident. It lowers the mean ALC down to about $\gamma=0.1$, but it also makes bad
groups more common: the share of groups with $B_3>0.3$ grows from 5.2\% at $\gamma=0.5$ to 7.4\% at $\gamma=0.2$. We
kept $\gamma=0.35$ while the subject has at most three labels, and $\gamma=0.2$ after that, when the posterior is
better informed.

\paragraph{Formative feedback.} Our seven earlier submissions scored between 0.164 and 0.228 on Codabench. Each
submission is scored on a new draw of about nine groups, so these numbers move by several hundredths from one draw to
the next, and we did not use them to rank models.

\section{What did not help}\label{sec:negative}
We tested each of these ideas with the same paired simulation and dropped them.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
\item Predicting budget 0 from item text and subject attributes with LightGBM (e5 embeddings, text statistics,
provider, release date, size, model name). The best $B_0$ was 0.248 against 0.250. Once the shift prior is centred,
the model already reaches the $B_0$ of the Bayes reference (0.2356).
\item Asking Qwen2.5-7B to rate item difficulty from 1 to 9. The rank correlation with measured difficulty inside a
benchmark was $-0.03$. Item contents rarely include a reference answer (3 benchmarks out of 92), so we could not
score the likelihood of the correct answer either.
\item Looking for the same item in other benchmarks. Fewer than 0.1\% of items have a match with cosine above 0.95.
\item Learning a projection of the embeddings for the kernel by maximising the marginal likelihood on training groups.
It was worse on held-out benchmarks, and a learned low-rank metric for the linear kernel did not help either.
\item Bigger item vectors (e5-base, e5-large, Qwen2.5-7B layers 14, 21 or 28) as the only kernel input. None beat
e5-small. With so few labels, the number of labels limits the model more than the quality of the vectors. Qwen2.5-14B
in place of the 7B model gained 0.0002 ALC for twice the memory.
\item Stacking with LightGBM on model outputs and label counts, and Platt scaling per budget. Both were worse on held-out
benchmarks, because calibration learned on some benchmarks does not carry over to others.
\item Smarter label choice: uncertainty, overlap with other subjects, embedding diversity, D-optimal design. No
significant difference from random order.
\item Learning the centre of the shift prior during the run, from the label rates of the other benchmarks in the
same draw (a hierarchical prior with a shared centre). It was worse in every fold, by 0.0001 to 0.0007 ALC depending
on the prior width of the centre, even in the fold whose benchmarks are hardest. The level of a subject on one
benchmark tells little about the level of other subjects on other benchmarks.
\item Weights for the averaging grid learned on training benchmarks. The posterior over the grid stays almost flat even
with 31 labels (weights from 0.026 to 0.048 over 27 points), so a learned prior is close to the uniform one.
\item A prior on subject ability from the same model name on other benchmarks. The out-of-fold correlation with
ability on held-out benchmarks was $-0.16$, and there was no gain. A ridge regression of the group level on all subject
metadata tokens did no better: its out-of-fold correlation with the group logit was between $-0.19$ and $+0.24$
depending on the subset, and on all groups it was worse than a constant. A prior on item difficulty inside a benchmark from a
ridge on centred Qwen states (Spearman 0.07) hurt $B_0$ and $B_1$ more than it helped later.
\item Numeric metadata (lengths, counts and similar fields) or text statistics as an extra kernel term. All versions
were 0.0001 to 0.0005 worse.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}
The simulator depends on how we read the draw rule. We matched it to the formative logs, and we checked every choice in
three settings for this reason. The centred shift prior helps because test groups have a low accuracy on average, as
in the training groups of the same size; on a test set dominated by easy benchmarks it would give back part of its
gain at budget 0, although it did not hurt in our large group setting. The Qwen parts need a GPU at test time; without
one the predictor still runs with the e5 model alone. The model assumes that item effects are the same for all
subjects of a benchmark, apart from the logistic link. Finally, with no labels the model predicts close to the
average level of a group, since the accuracy of a subject on a new benchmark is hard to predict from the data we have.
