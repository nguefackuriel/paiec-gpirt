\begin{abstract}
Running every AI system on every benchmark costs a lot, so it helps to predict how a system will do on a new benchmark
from only a few of its results. The Predictive AI Evaluation Competition (PAIEC) asks for a predictor that gives the
chance that a system answers an item correctly, when the benchmark is not in the training data and labels are shown a
few at a time (0, 1, 3, 7, 15 and 31 per system and benchmark). This is hard because, with so few labels, most of the
error comes from not knowing how well the system does on the benchmark as a whole. To address this challenge, we
design GP-IRT, which fits a logistic item response model on each test benchmark at test time, using the labels of all
systems on that benchmark. Item effects have a Gaussian process prior over item embeddings, so items with similar text
get similar difficulty, and predictions are averaged over a grid of prior scales, with weights from the Laplace
marginal likelihood. The prior on the benchmark level is centred on the accuracy of groups of the size used at test
time, which is about 0.36 in training and 0.35 in the test groups we recovered from formative feedback. We test GP-IRT
with a simulator of the official protocol on held-out benchmarks. GP-IRT reaches a Brier ALC of 0.1797, against 0.1869
for a group mean and 0.25 for a constant 0.5. Centring the prior gives the largest single gain: it lowers the Brier
score at budget 0 from 0.248 to 0.236 and the Brier ALC by 0.002.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Benchmarks are the main way to compare AI systems, but running a new system on many benchmarks takes time and money.
Predictive evaluation tries to guess the result of a system on an item before running it, from results we already
have~\citep{paiec2026}. PAIEC asks this question in a hard setting: the test benchmarks never appear in training, and
the predictor sees only a few labels on each of them. The training data is the AI Measurement Data
Bank~\citep{measurementdb2026}, and predictions are scored with the Brier score~\citep{glenn1950verification}, averaged
over label budgets.

\textbf{Limitation of existing solutions:} Item response theory (IRT)~\citep{lord2012applications} gives the chance of
a correct answer from the ability of a subject and the difficulty of an item, and it has been used to evaluate language
models on a few items. For example, tinyBenchmarks~\citep{polo2024tinybenchmarks} estimates the accuracy of a new model
from a small set of items, with item parameters learned from many models already run on the same benchmark. In PAIEC
the test benchmarks are new, so the item parameters are not known in advance and must be learned at test time from the
few labels shown. Simple baselines, such as the mean of the labels shown for a group, do not use the labels of other
systems or the text of the items.

\textbf{Challenges:} New benchmarks with very few labels raise four problems:
\ch{1} \emph{Unknown benchmark level:} with zero or one label, most of the error comes from the overall level of the
system on the benchmark, not from single items.
\ch{2} \emph{Few labels per item:} most items have no label, so their difficulty must be learned from the labels of
other systems and from the item text.
\ch{3} \emph{Benchmarks differ a lot:} on some benchmarks a system gets almost every item right or almost every item
wrong, while on others items differ little, so one prior does not fit all of them.
\ch{4} \emph{No test benchmark to choose a model:} the formative feedback on Codabench~\citep{xu2022codabench} is
computed on a new random draw for each submission, so it cannot be used to compare models.

\textbf{Contribution:} In this paper, we make the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
\item To address challenge \ch{1}, GP-IRT puts a prior on the benchmark shift that is centred on the level of training
groups of realistic size. The formative logs show that the test groups have the same low accuracy (0.35 on average).
\item To address challenge \ch{2}, GP-IRT uses the labels of all systems on a benchmark together and gives the item
effects a Gaussian process prior~\citep{rasmussen2006gaussian}. Its kernel combines the similarity of text embeddings
and of metadata tokens with a linear kernel on embeddings centred within the benchmark.
\item To address challenge \ch{3}, GP-IRT fits the model for a grid of prior scales and averages the predictions, with
weights from the Laplace marginal likelihood, as in Bayesian model averaging~\citep{hoeting1999bayesian}.
\item To address challenge \ch{4}, we built a simulator of the official protocol, with 5-fold cross-validation over
benchmarks and paired comparisons, and made every design choice with it. We release the code and the simulator.
\end{itemize}

\section{Task and Data}
\textbf{Task:} A subject is an AI system with fixed settings. An item is one question, with its text, some metadata
and an anonymous benchmark ID. A group is one subject on one benchmark. In each scored group with at least 80 items,
the items are split in two halves: labels are shown from one half, and the other half is scored. Labels come in nested
steps of 0, 1, 3, 7, 15 and 31 per group. The predictor sees the labels of every group, including groups it does not
predict. The score is the Brier ALC, $0.1B_0+0.2B_1+0.2B_3+0.2B_7+0.2B_{15}+0.1B_{31}$, averaged over groups.

\textbf{Training data:} We use only the public training pool \texttt{aims-foundations/measurement-db} (revision
\texttt{54074453}). We keep benchmarks with binary item-level answers and \texttt{release = public}, which gives 160
benchmarks. Of these, 92 have at least one group with 80 to 1000 items, for a total of 10{,}208 groups, 154{,}628
items and 4.3M subject and item cells. Each cell holds $n$ trials and $k$ successes. No other evaluation data is used.

\section{GP-IRT: Item Response Prediction Fit at Test Time}
\subsection{Overview}
For each test benchmark, GP-IRT works in five steps. It first collects all labels shown on the benchmark, from any
subject (Step~1). It then builds item vectors from the item text and metadata (Step~2). It fits a logistic item
response model with a Gaussian process prior on the item effects, once for each point of a grid of prior scales
(Step~3). It averages the predictions of the grid, with weights from the Laplace marginal likelihood (Step~4).
Finally, it returns the chance that the subject answers each target item correctly (Step~5). The fits are redone each
time new labels arrive.

\subsection{Model}
Fix a benchmark $b$ and let $\mathcal{L}$ be the labels shown on $b$, from any subject. For subject $s$ and item $t$,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{logit} P(y_{st}=1) = c_t + m + a_s + d_t,\quad
a_s\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_a^2),\; d\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,\sigma_d^2 K).
\end{equation}
Here $c_t$ is a fixed offset from a prior model (Section~\ref{sec:prior}), $m$ is a shift for the benchmark, $a_s$ is
a shift for the subject, and $d_t$ is the item effect, shared by all subjects of the benchmark. The prior on $m$ is
given in Section~\ref{sec:shift}. The item kernel is
\begin{equation}
K = (1-\rho_\ell)\big[\rho\,e^{(\cos(e_i,e_j)-1)/\ell} + \rho_f\,\kappa_{\text{tok}}(i,j) + (1-\rho-\rho_f)\,\mathbb{1}[i=j]\big]
  + \rho_\ell\,\frac{\tilde e_i^\top \tilde e_j}{\overline{\|\tilde e\|^2}}.
\end{equation}
$e$ is the normalised \texttt{multilingual-e5-small}~\citep{wang2024multilingual} embedding of the item text and
metadata. $\kappa_{\text{tok}}$ is the cosine similarity of \texttt{key=value} metadata tokens, counting only tokens
found on at least two labeled items. $\tilde e = e-\bar e_{\mathcal{L}}$ is the embedding minus its mean over the
labeled items of the benchmark. The last term acts as a Bayesian linear regression of item difficulty on the
embedding, learned for each benchmark from its labels.

\subsection{Inference}
We find the posterior mode with Newton's method. The Hessian block of the subject effects is diagonal, so we remove it
with a Schur complement. What is left is a block for $m$ and the labeled item effects, of size at most the number of
distinct labeled items. One Newton step costs $O(|\mathcal{L}| + n_s n_{\mathcal{L}} + n_{\mathcal{L}}^3)$. The
Laplace posterior~\citep[Chapter~3]{rasmussen2006gaussian} gives a mean $\mu$ and a variance $v$ of the logit for any
target item, labeled or not, through the Gaussian process conditional of $d_t$ given $d_{\mathcal{L}}$. We predict
$\sigma\big(\mu/\sqrt{1+\pi\gamma v/8}\big)$. With $\gamma=1$ this is the usual probit approximation of the predictive
integral~\citep{mackay1992evidence}. We use $\gamma=0.35$ while the target subject has at most three labels on the
benchmark and $\gamma=0.2$ after that (Section~\ref{sec:risk}).

\subsection{Averaging over Prior Scales}
We fit the model for every combination of $\sigma_a\in\{0.4,0.8,1.6\}$, $\sigma_d\in\{0.8,1.6,3.2\}$ and the three
parts of the shift prior below, with $\rho=0.73$, $\rho_f=0.17$, $\ell=0.12$ and $\rho_\ell=0.4$. The predicted
chances are averaged with weights proportional to the Laplace marginal likelihood. This is done for each benchmark and
each set of labels separately.

\subsection{Prior on the Benchmark Shift}\label{sec:shift}
The shift $m$ sets the overall level of a subject on a new benchmark. Its prior mixes three normal distributions with
equal weights,
\begin{equation}
m \sim \tfrac13\big[\mathcal{N}(\mu_0, 0.8^2) + \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, 1.6^2) + \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, 3.2^2)\big],
\qquad \mu_0=-0.6,
\end{equation}
and each part is one more axis of the averaging grid. The wide part lets the model follow benchmarks where a subject
gets almost everything right or wrong, and the narrow part keeps predictions stable with one or two labels. The centre
$\mu_0$ matters more than the scales. In training groups of realistic size, the mean logit accuracy is $-0.87$, with a
standard deviation of 1.8, so a prior centred at zero starts every new benchmark from an accuracy that is too high. We
chose $\mu_0$ by cross-validation (Section~\ref{sec:results}). The chosen value lies between zero and the training
mean, because the offset $c_t$, whose mean is negative, already accounts for part of the low level, and the probit
correction pulls predictions toward 0.5.

\subsection{Prior across Benchmarks and Second Model}\label{sec:prior}
We take hidden states of Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct~\citep{qwen2025qwen25technicalreport} (layer 21 of 28, mean over tokens,
standardised) and fit a ridge regression to the mean logit accuracy of each item, giving the same weight to every
training benchmark. The output is $z_t$, and we set $c_t = 0.3\,z_t$. With no labels, the prediction is the prior
predictive, averaged over the parts of the shift prior. The final predictor is the average of two copies of the model
that differ only in the item vectors used by the kernel: e5-small embeddings in one, and Qwen hidden states in the
other.

\subsection{Implementation}
Labels are taken in the platform's default random order (Section~\ref{sec:negative}). Without a GPU, or if Qwen cannot
be loaded or fails during the run, the predictor uses the e5 model alone, with no offset $c_t$. In simulation, this
costs only 0.0003 ALC in the realistic setting. A prediction takes 15 to 110~ms on one A100 GPU.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:sim}
We built GP-IRT in Python. The Qwen hidden states took about 2.5 hours on one A100 80GB GPU, and the simulations ran
on 30 CPU cores, where 1000 episodes of the realistic setting take 10 to 20 minutes.

\textbf{Simulator:} Test benchmarks never appear in training, so we made every choice with a simulator of the official
protocol and 5-fold cross-validation over benchmarks. Groups are added to a draw one at a time. Groups whose benchmark
or model name is already in the draw come last, and ties are broken at random. We stop when the draw would pass 1000
subject and item pairs. In each group, the distinct items are split at random, and the odd item goes to the label
pool. Labels come in random order, with one sampled trial per item, and every group sees the labels of all other
groups.

\textbf{Settings:} We use three settings. In the realistic setting, our main one, only groups with at most 200 items
are allowed. A draw then has on average 6.9 groups, 5.9 benchmarks and 142 items per group. The large group setting
allows all groups with 80 to 1000 items and gives about 3 groups per draw. In the many subjects setting, the limit
counts distinct items, so a benchmark can have hundreds of subjects.

\textbf{Baselines:} We compare GP-IRT with a constant 0.5, the mean of the labels shown for the group with a
Beta(1,1) prior, and the Bayes predictor of the group accuracy from the group's own labels, with the accuracies of
training groups from other folds as its prior (groups of 80 to 200 items, in all three columns). We also compare with
IRT models without item effects and with independent item effects.

\textbf{Metrics:} We compute the exact Brier score of each group over all its response rows, apply the ALC weights,
and average over groups and then over episodes. Two models are always compared on the same episodes, with the same
splits and the same labels, and we report the paired mean difference with its standard error. Settings were chosen on
one seed and checked again on episodes drawn with another seed.

\textbf{Formative feedback:} Each Codabench submission is scored on a new draw of eight or nine groups, and the scoring
log gives the Brier score and the calibration error (ECE) of each group at each budget. Since the draw changes, these
logs say little about which model is better, but they do describe the test groups. A draw has about nine groups from
seven benchmarks, with 88 to 152 items per group, which matches the realistic setting. At budget 0 the prediction is
the same for all items of a group, so the ECE is the distance between this prediction and the group accuracy, and the
Brier score tells on which side of the prediction the accuracy lies. In this way we recovered the accuracy of 27 test
groups, with a mean of 0.35. Fig.~\ref{fig:acc} compares them with the training groups. The test groups look like the
training groups of realistic size (mean 0.36 per group, 0.39 per benchmark), and not like the training pool as a whole
(0.49 per benchmark), where large benchmarks with many subjects count the most. The logs led us to the realistic
setting and to the level of the shift prior. Every parameter, including the centre of that prior, was then chosen by
cross-validation on the training pool.

\textbf{RQ1: How well does GP-IRT predict results on new benchmarks compared with the baselines?:}
Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the Brier scores of GP-IRT and the baselines. GP-IRT has the lowest ALC in the realistic
and many subjects settings (0.1797 and 0.1693), against 0.1869 and 0.2133 for the group mean. With large groups, it is
within 0.0003 of the best row, which is inside the noise of that setting. The Bayes reference is useful here. At
budget 0 it predicts the mean training accuracy, which is the best constant for the training data. Its $B_0$ is
0.2356, and GP-IRT reaches the same value, so there is little left to gain at budget 0 without information on single
items. From budget 1 on, GP-IRT beats this reference by 0.004 to 0.008 at every budget. This gain comes from the
labels of other subjects on the same benchmark and from the item kernel. Fig.~\ref{fig:curves} shows the curves.
Two oracles give limits in the realistic setting. If the accuracy of each group were known, a constant prediction per
group would score 0.1503. If the accuracy of each item for each subject were known, the Brier score would be 0.011.
Our $B_{31}$ of 0.1494 is just below the first oracle: with 31 labels, the model knows the group level well and gets a
little more from the items, but the gap to the second oracle shows how much item information it still misses.
On Codabench, the final model (submission 1018959) scored 0.182 Brier ALC, with an ECE of 0.139. Each submission is
scored on a new draw of eight or nine groups, so this score can move by several hundredths from one draw to the next.

\textbf{RQ2: What does each part of the model add?:} Table~\ref{tab:main} adds the parts of the model one at a time,
on the same 1000 episodes in every row. The paired standard error of the difference between two rows is about
$10^{-4}$ in the realistic setting. The centred shift prior gives the largest step in the realistic setting
($-0.0022$, from 0.1819 to 0.1797), almost all of it at budgets 0 and 1. The centred linear kernel gives the largest
step with large groups ($-0.0025$) and helps from budget 7 on. Most other rows change the ALC by less than 0.001.

\textbf{RQ3: How does the prior on the benchmark shift change the results?:} We compared centres and scales on 300
paired episodes of the realistic setting, against the model of the row before the final one in Table~\ref{tab:main}.
Centres of $-0.4$, $-0.6$ and $-0.8$ lower the ALC by 0.0018 to 0.0022, all with a standard error near 0.0002, while
$-1.2$ gives only 0.0010. The scales matter less: $(1, 2, 4)$, $(0.8, 1.6, 3.2)$ and $(1.5, 3)$ are within 0.0001 of
each other. Gaussian mixtures with two or three parts fit directly to the training group accuracies did about as well
($-0.0023$), so we kept the simpler prior. Once the prior is centred, the Qwen offset adds almost nothing: setting
$c_t=0$ changes the ALC by $+0.00001\pm0.00007$. On 1000 new episodes drawn with another seed, the final model beats
the row before it by $0.0022\pm0.0001$ ALC, and its Brier score is lower by 0.012 at budget 0 and by 0.004 at
budget 1. In the large group setting, where the training accuracy is higher, the centred prior neither helps nor
hurts: the change is $-0.0008\pm0.0005$ on the episodes used for the choice and $+0.0002\pm0.0003$ on new episodes.

\textbf{RQ4: How risky are predictions with very few labels?:}\label{sec:risk} With one to three labels, a predictor
can move away from the truth. In simulation, $B_3>B_0$ happens for about a quarter of the groups, and in 53 groups of
formative logs it happened for 24.5\%, so the simulator matches the real behaviour on this point. A smaller variance
scale $\gamma$ makes predictions more confident. It lowers the mean ALC down to about $\gamma=0.1$, but it also makes
bad groups more common: the share of groups with $B_3>0.3$ grows from 5.2\% at $\gamma=0.5$ to 7.4\% at
$\gamma=0.2$. We use $\gamma=0.35$ while the subject has at most three labels, and $\gamma=0.2$ after that, when the
posterior knows more.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:negative}
While GP-IRT does better than the baselines in our simulation, it has some limits:

\textbf{Simulator:} The results depend on how we read the draw rule of the competition. We matched the simulator to
the formative logs, and for this reason we checked every choice in three settings.

\textbf{Level of the test groups:} The centred shift prior helps because test groups have a low accuracy on average,
like training groups of the same size. On a test set made mostly of easy benchmarks, it would lose part of its gain at
budget 0, although it did not hurt in our large group setting.

\textbf{Model:} The model assumes that item effects are the same for all subjects of a benchmark. With no labels, it
predicts close to the average level of a group, since the accuracy of a subject on a new benchmark is hard to predict
from the data we have. The Qwen parts need a GPU at test time; without one, the predictor still runs with the e5 model
alone.

\textbf{What did not help:} We tested these ideas with the same paired simulation and dropped them.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
\item Predicting budget 0 from item text and subject details (provider, release date, size, model name) with
LightGBM~\citep{ke2017lightgbm}. The best $B_0$ was 0.248, against 0.250, while the centred prior reaches 0.2356.
\item Asking Qwen2.5-7B to rate item difficulty from 1 to 9. The rank correlation with measured difficulty inside a
benchmark was $-0.03$.
\item Larger item vectors (e5-base, e5-large, other Qwen layers). None beat e5-small: with so few labels, the number of
labels limits the model more than the quality of the vectors.
\item A prior on subject ability from the same model on other benchmarks. Its out-of-fold correlation with ability on
held-out benchmarks was $-0.16$, and it gave no gain.
\item Learned calibration (stacking with LightGBM, Platt scaling~\citep{platt2000probabilities} per budget). Both were
worse on held-out benchmarks, because calibration learned on some benchmarks does not carry over to others.
\item Choosing labels by uncertainty, overlap with other subjects, embedding diversity or D-optimal design. None was
clearly different from random order.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Item response theory:} IRT~\citep{lord2012applications} models a correct answer from the ability of a subject
and the difficulty of an item. tinyBenchmarks~\citep{polo2024tinybenchmarks} uses IRT to estimate the accuracy of a
language model from a small set of items, with item parameters learned from models already run on the same benchmark.
GP-IRT instead learns the item effects of a new benchmark at test time, and links items through a Gaussian process
prior on their embeddings.

\textbf{Gaussian process classification:} GP-IRT uses the Laplace approximation for Gaussian process
classification~\citep{rasmussen2006gaussian} and the probit approximation of the predictive
integral~\citep{mackay1992evidence}. Averaging predictions over prior scales with weights from the marginal likelihood
follows Bayesian model averaging~\citep{hoeting1999bayesian}.

\section{Conclusion}
In this paper, we designed GP-IRT, a method that predicts whether an AI system answers an item of a new benchmark
correctly from a few labels. GP-IRT fits a logistic item response model on each benchmark at test time, uses the labels
of all subjects together, links items through a Gaussian process prior on their embeddings, and averages over prior
scales. In a simulator of the official protocol on held-out benchmarks, GP-IRT reaches a Brier ALC of 0.1797, and
centring the prior on the benchmark level gives the largest single gain. Most of the remaining error is at the level
of the group, which is hard to predict for a new benchmark from the data we have.

\textbf{Reproducibility:} The code is at \url{https://github.com/nguefackuriel/paiec-gpirt}. Its README gives the
steps to rerun everything: download and preparation of the training pool, embeddings, the simulator, the scripts
behind each table and figure, and the build of the submission. Pretrained models:
\texttt{intfloat/multilingual-e5-small} and \texttt{Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct}. Training data: only
\texttt{aims-foundations/measurement-db}.

\textbf{Use of AI assistants:} The code, the experiments and drafts of this report were made with the help of an LLM
coding assistant (Claude Code, Anthropic). All results come from the code in the repository.
